  • First void by 24 h. First meconium by 24–48 h.

Red flags that need a call now
  • Jaundice in the first 24 h
  • Central cyanosis, grunting, retractions, RR persistently >60
  • Bilious (green) emesis
  • Temp instability (low temp is the more common sepsis sign in newborns)
  • Lethargy, poor suck, high-pitched cry, apnea
  • Boggy scalp swelling that crosses sutures and keeps growing (subgaleal)
  • No void by 24 h or no stool by 48 h
  • Failed CCHD screen

Scored at 1 and 5 min. If the 5-min score is <7, keep scoring every 5 min up to 20 min. Apgar is not used to decide whether to resuscitate.

Periods of reactivity (normal transition)
  • First reactivity (0–30 min): alert, rooting, HR up to ~180, RR up to ~80, some crackles or flaring. Best window for skin-to-skin and first breastfeed.
  • Sleep / decreased responsiveness (30 min – 2 h): HR and RR settle; hard to wake. Good time for vitals and meds.
  • Second reactivity (2–8 h): awake again, mucus, gagging, may pass meconium. Watch for spit-up and color changes.
Thermoregulation
  • Heat loss routes: evaporation (wet skin, bath), conduction (cold scale or hands), convection (drafts), radiation (cold window or wall).
  • Newborns make heat from brown fat (non-shivering thermogenesis). This burns glucose and oxygen, so cold stress leads to hypoglycemia, acidosis and respiratory distress.
  • Fixes: skin-to-skin, dry and hat, warm blankets, radiant warmer on servo.
  • Bath: delay at least 6–8 h (WHO suggests ≥24 h) and only once temp is stable.
Apnea vs periodic breathing

Periodic breathing: pauses <20 sec with no color or HR change. Normal.

Apnea: pause ≥20 sec, or a shorter pause with bradycardia (<100), desaturation or color change. Stimulate first; PPV if no response. Notify provider and think sepsis, hypoglycemia, hypothermia, seizure.

Pain scales

NIPS (Neonatal Infant Pain Scale): facial expression, cry, breathing pattern, arms, legs, state of arousal. Scored 0–7; >3 suggests moderate pain and >4 severe.

N-PASS is also common and covers sedation too. Use whichever your unit charts.

Non-drug comfort: swaddling, facilitated tuck, skin-to-skin, breastfeeding during the procedure, non-nutritive sucking, 24% oral sucrose per protocol.

Head & scalp
  • Anterior fontanelle: diamond, soft and flat, roughly 1–4 cm. Closes 12–18 mo. Posterior: triangle, fingertip size, closes by ~2 mo.
  • Bulging when calm = raised ICP. Sunken = dehydration.
  • Molding: overlapping sutures after vaginal birth. Resolves in days.

Subgaleal: serial head circumference, frequent vitals, call provider immediately. Watch for tachycardia, pallor, falling Hct.
Face, eyes, ears, nose, mouth
  • Eyes: red reflex in both eyes. Absent or white reflex needs ophthalmology. Subconjunctival hemorrhage is a normal birth finding.
  • Ears: top of the pinna lines up with the outer canthus. Low-set ears suggest a syndrome. Preauricular pits or tags: flag for hearing screen follow-up.
  • Nose: newborns breathe through the nose. Cyanosis that improves with crying suggests choanal atresia; check that a catheter passes each naris if concerned.
  • Mouth: look at and palpate the palate for a cleft. Epstein pearls (small white cysts) are normal. Note natal teeth, tongue-tie, and suck strength.
  • Asymmetric cry or facial droop = facial nerve palsy (often after forceps).
Neck, chest, lungs, heart
  • Clavicles: crepitus, a lump or an asymmetric Moro suggests fracture. Most common in LGA babies and after shoulder dystocia.
  • Chest: breast buds and even "witch's milk" are maternal hormone effects. Supernumerary nipples are a normal variant.
  • Respiratory distress signs: RR >60, grunting, nasal flaring, retractions, see-saw breathing, cyanosis.
  • Heart: PMI at the 4th intercostal space, left of the midclavicular line. Soft murmurs are common in the first 24–48 h while the PDA closes. Report any murmur.
  • Femoral pulses should be equal to brachial. Weak or absent femorals suggest coarctation.
Abdomen, cord, GU, anus
  • Soft and rounded. A scaphoid abdomen with respiratory distress suggests diaphragmatic hernia.
  • Liver edge 1–2 cm below the right costal margin is normal.
  • Cord: 2 arteries + 1 vein (AVA). A single umbilical artery is linked to renal and other anomalies; report it.
  • Female: swollen labia, white mucus or a small amount of blood (pseudomenses) are hormone effects.
  • Male: both testes descended; urethral meatus at the tip. Hypospadias means no circumcision until urology weighs in. Hydrocele transilluminates.
  • Anus: check patency and position. Document first meconium.
Back, spine, hips
  • Sacral dimple: reassuring if small, within the gluteal crease and base visible. Report if >5 mm, >2.5 cm above the anus, or paired with a hair tuft, hemangioma, skin tag or discoloration.
  • Barlow: adduct the hip and push posteriorly. Tests whether the hip can be dislocated.
  • Ortolani: abduct and lift the greater trochanter. A "clunk" means a dislocated hip is reducing.
  • Other signs: uneven knee heights (Galeazzi), asymmetric thigh folds, limited abduction.
  • DDH risk factors: breech, female, family history, first-born.
Extremities & brachial plexus
  • Count fingers and toes; check for webbing. A single transverse palmar crease can be a normal variant or linked to trisomy 21.
  • Erb palsy (C5–C6): arm limp, adducted and internally rotated, wrist flexed ("waiter's tip"). Grasp is intact.
  • Klumpke palsy (C8–T1): hand weakness, claw hand, absent grasp.
  • Positional foot deformities correct with gentle range of motion. Clubfoot does not.

For jitteriness, check glucose (and calcium if ordered). Also consider withdrawal.

Withdrawal: Eat, Sleep, Console Newer

Many units have replaced Finnegan scoring for opioid-exposed newborns with Eat, Sleep, Console (ESC). It is function-based and puts non-drug care first.

  • Eat: can the baby breastfeed ≥10 min or take an age-appropriate bottle volume?
  • Sleep: can the baby sleep ≥1 h after feeding?
  • Console: can the baby be consoled within 10 min?

A "no" triggers a huddle to add non-drug support first (parent at bedside, skin-to-skin, low stimulation, swaddle, on-demand feeds). Medication is considered only if non-drug care is maximized and the infant still struggles.

Other withdrawal signs: high-pitched cry, tremors, hypertonia, sneezing, yawning, loose stools, poor feeding, skin excoriation.

Hands-on skills

Steps as a memory prompt. Follow your unit's procedure and supplies.

NG / OG tube placement

Size
Term: 5–8 Fr (5 or 6.5 Fr is common for feeding; 8 Fr for decompression). Late preterm: 5 Fr.

Measure
NG: nose → earlobe → midpoint between xiphoid and umbilicus (NEMU). OG: corner of mouth → earlobe → same midpoint. NEMU is more accurate than the older nose-ear-xiphoid method.

  1. Hand hygiene, gather supplies, swaddle. Monitor HR and SpO₂.
  2. Head neutral or slightly flexed. Lubricate the tip with sterile water.
  3. Insert gently along the floor of the nose (NG) or over the tongue (OG). Offer a pacifier to encourage swallowing.
  4. Stop and pull back for bradycardia, desaturation, coughing, color change or resistance. These are vagal responses or signs of entering the airway.
  5. Advance to the measured mark. Secure with tape or a securement device. Document the cm mark at the naris or lip.
  6. Verify: X-ray is the gold standard for initial placement. Gastric aspirate pH ≤5 supports gastric placement (per unit policy). The "whoosh" auscultation test is not reliable.
  7. Before every feed: check the cm mark, check for residual per order, and look for respiratory distress.
Peripheral IV (PIV)

Supplies
24 G catheter, T-connector primed with NS, transparent dressing, tape, tourniquet or rubber band, antiseptic per policy, sucrose, transilluminator or vein finder.

Sites
Dorsal hand, forearm, antecubital, saphenous (ankle), dorsal foot, scalp (only per policy and after talking with parents).

  1. Comfort first: swaddle other limbs, 24% sucrose ~2 min before, a second person to hold if available.
  2. Transilluminate to map the vein. Apply the tourniquet or gently squeeze the limb.
  3. Clean per policy. CHG use in newborns varies by age and gestation; follow your unit.
  4. Bevel up, 10–30° angle. Watch for flashback (it can be slow with a 24 G).
  5. Lower the angle, advance slightly, thread the catheter off the needle. Release the tourniquet.
  6. Connect the primed T-connector, flush slowly with 0.5–1 mL NS. Watch for blanching or swelling.
  7. Secure with a clear dressing so the site stays visible. Avoid circumferential tape.
INS standard: no more than 2 attempts per clinician and 4 total before escalating.
Check the site at least hourly (common neonatal standard) for swelling, blanching, coolness, leaking or a firm tract. Stop the infusion at the first sign of infiltration.
Heel stick & newborn screen card
  1. Use the lateral or medial plantar edge of the heel. Avoid the back curve and center (calcaneus and nerve risk).
  2. Warm the heel 3–5 min to improve flow.
  3. Clean, let it dry, use an automated lancet.
  4. Wipe away the first drop. Gentle squeeze-and-release; hard milking hemolyzes the sample (falsely high K⁺).

Newborn screen card

  • Collect at 24–48 h of age (after 24 h of feeding is ideal).
  • Fill each circle from one side with one large drop until it soaks through. Don't layer, and don't touch the filter paper.
  • Dry flat, away from heat and sunlight, 3–4 h before packaging.
  • Repeat is often required if drawn before 24 h, after a transfusion, or during TPN. Follow your state program.
IM injection
  • Site: vastus lateralis (anterolateral thigh, middle third).
  • Needle: 25 G, 5/8 inch, 90° angle. Stabilize the thigh.
  • Keep to about 0.5 mL per site in the newborn. Give different products in different thighs, or space them ≥1 inch apart in the same thigh.
  • Breastfeeding or skin-to-skin during the injection reduces pain.
CCHD pulse oximetry screen

At ≥24 h of age (or just before discharge if earlier). Measure the right hand and either foot.

ResultCriteria
Pass≥95% in hand or foot and difference ≤3%
Repeat once in 1 h90–94% in both, or difference >3%
Fail<90% in either, or still in repeat range on the second check

A fail means provider evaluation and echocardiogram. The 2020 AAP update cut the algorithm from two repeats to one.

Circumcision care
  • Check for bleeding frequently in the first hours (per policy). Apply gentle pressure with gauze; call if it persists.
  • Gomco or Mogen clamp: petroleum jelly on gauze at each diaper change for several days.
  • Plastibell: no petroleum needed. Ring falls off in 5–8 days. Report if it slips onto the shaft.
  • Yellow film on the glans on day 2–3 is normal healing tissue, not pus.
  • Document the first void after the procedure.
Phototherapy
  • Maximize exposed skin. A diaper can stay on. Cover the eyes; check the shield position regularly.
  • Intensive phototherapy: irradiance ≥30 µW/cm²/nm over as much body surface as possible.
  • Monitor temp, hydration, stool count and weight. Loose green stools are expected.
  • Skin color is not a guide once lights are on; follow serum levels.
  • Keep feeding. Interruptions for breastfeeding are generally fine unless bili is near exchange level.
Car seat tolerance screen

For infants born <37 weeks (and others per policy). Baby sits in their own car seat for 90–120 min, or the length of the ride home if longer.

Fail (common criteria): apnea ≥20 s, HR <80 for ≥10 s, or SpO₂ <88% for ≥10 s. Notify the provider.

Medications

Common nursery and early-sepsis meds for term and late-preterm infants. Always check the order and your neonatal formulary; doses shift with gestational and postnatal age.

Vitamin K (phytonadione)

Dose: 1 mg IM once, within about 6 h of birth. Neonatal concentration 2 mg/mL = 0.5 mL. (Preterm <1 kg: 0.3–0.5 mg/kg.)

Why: prevents vitamin K deficiency bleeding. Newborns are born with low stores and breast milk is low in vitamin K.

Refusals: late VKDB (weeks 2–12) can present as intracranial bleeding. Educate and document per policy. No FDA-approved oral product exists in the US.

Erythromycin ophthalmic ointment 0.5%

Dose: 1 cm ribbon in each lower conjunctival sac, inner to outer canthus, within 1–2 h of birth. Don't wipe off for 1 min.

Why: prevents gonococcal ophthalmia neonatorum. It can be delayed briefly to allow bonding and the first feed.

Hepatitis B vaccine & HBIG Changed

Dose: 0.5 mL IM (Engerix-B 10 mcg or Recombivax HB 5 mcg).

RSV monoclonal antibody New

Given during RSV season (Oct 1 – Mar 31 in most of the continental US), ideally during the birth stay.

ProductDose

Can be given the same day as other newborn injections.

Oral sucrose 24%

Use: short procedural pain (heel stick, IV, IM, NG).

Dose: per unit protocol. Term infants commonly get about 0.5–2 mL, dripped on the front of the tongue or inside the cheek ~2 min before the procedure, often with a pacifier.

Dextrose gel 40%

Dose: 0.5 mL/kg (200 mg/kg) to the buccal mucosa. Dry the cheek, massage it in, then feed.

Use: asymptomatic hypoglycemia in infants ≥35 weeks, usually in the first 48 h. Recheck glucose ~30 min after. Most protocols allow a limited number of doses before IV dextrose.

IV dextrose (D10W)

Bolus (mini-bolus): 2 mL/kg of D10W IV (200 mg/kg), then a continuous infusion.

Maintenance: start around GIR 4–6 mg/kg/min. D10W at 80 mL/kg/day ≈ GIR 5.5.

GIR formula: (dextrose % × mL/kg/day) ÷ 144.

Never give concentrated dextrose (D25/D50) peripherally to a newborn.

Labs & what you'll see

Normal ranges for term newborns and how abnormal values tend to show up at the bedside. Ranges vary by lab.

Glucose

Who gets screened: SGA, LGA, infant of diabetic mother, late preterm (34–36 6/7). Also anyone with symptoms, hypothermia, sepsis concerns, or poor feeding.

AgeGlucoseAction (AAP 2011)
0–4 h<25Feed, recheck in 1 h. Still <25 → IV dextrose. 25–40 → refeed or IV as needed
4–24 h<35Feed, recheck in 1 h. Still <35 → IV dextrose. 35–45 → refeed or IV as needed
Target≥45 pre-feedPediatric Endocrine Society: aim >50 in the first 48 h and >60 after 48 h

At the bedside: jitteriness, poor feeding, lethargy, weak or high-pitched cry, hypothermia, tachypnea, apnea, cyanosis, sweating, hypotonia, seizures. Many low babies have no symptoms at all.

Point-of-care meters are less accurate at low values. Send a serum glucose to confirm a critical low, but don't delay treatment waiting for it.

Bilirubin Changed
  • Physiologic jaundice appears after 24 h and peaks around day 3–5 in term infants.
  • Jaundice before 24 h is always pathologic. Think hemolysis (ABO or Rh), sepsis.
  • Jaundice spreads head to toe. Visual estimates are unreliable, especially in darker skin; use TcB or TSB.
  • AAP 2022 guideline: phototherapy thresholds are based on gestational age, hours of life, and neurotoxicity risk factors. The old Bhutani risk-zone chart is no longer used for decisions. Use the AAP curves or BiliTool.
  • Neurotoxicity risk factors: GA <38 wk, albumin <3.0 g/dL, isoimmune hemolytic disease (positive DAT), G6PD deficiency or other hemolysis, sepsis, significant clinical instability in the past 24 h.
  • Get a TSB if TcB is within 3 mg/dL of the phototherapy threshold or ≥15 mg/dL.
  • Direct (conjugated) bilirubin >1.0 mg/dL is abnormal and needs follow-up (cholestasis, biliary atresia). Look for pale stools and dark urine.
Acute bilirubin encephalopathy: early lethargy, low tone, poor suck; then high-pitched cry, arching (opisthotonus), backward neck arching (retrocollis), fever. Emergency.

CRP & blood culture
  • CRP: normal <1 mg/dL (<10 mg/L). It rises 6–8 h after infection starts and peaks at 36–48 h. Two normal values 24 h apart help rule out sepsis.

Blood type, DAT & maternal labs to review
  • Mom type O or Rh negative → infant blood type and DAT (Coombs) per policy.
  • DAT positive = antibody-coated red cells, higher risk of hemolysis and early, fast-rising bilirubin. It is a neurotoxicity risk factor in the AAP bili curves.
  • Always review mom's chart: blood type, GBS status and whether she got adequate prophylaxis (penicillin, ampicillin or cefazolin ≥4 h before delivery), HBsAg, HIV, syphilis, rubella, hep C, rupture-of-membranes time, maternal fever, substance exposure, diabetes.
  • Congenital syphilis cases have risen sharply in the past several years. Many states now require third-trimester and delivery testing.

Feeding & output

Couplet teaching takes up much of a nursery shift.

Standard formula is 20 kcal/oz. Goal for most term infants is about 100–120 kcal/kg/day once feeding is established.

Breastfeeding
  • 8–12 feeds per 24 h, on cue. Don't let a baby go longer than ~3 h in the first days if they aren't waking.
  • Early cues: stirring, mouth opening, rooting, hands to mouth. Crying is a late cue.
  • Good latch: wide mouth, flanged lips, more areola visible above the top lip than below, chin touching breast, audible swallows, no pain after the first few sucks.
  • LATCH score (0–10): Latch, Audible swallowing, Type of nipple, Comfort, Hold.
  • Sleepy baby: skin-to-skin, unwrap, diaper change, hand expression onto the nipple.
  • Supplement only with a medical indication or informed parent choice, per Baby-Friendly practices.
Bottle feeding
  • Paced feeding: baby upright, bottle horizontal, pauses every few swallows. Helps late preterms and prevents overfeeding.
  • Burp midway and at the end.
  • Watch for stress cues: fingers splayed, eyebrows raised, milk leaking from mouth, color change, desaturation.
Weight loss tool

The NEWT (Newborn Weight Tool, newbornweight.org) plots weight loss by hours of age and delivery type against population percentiles. Above the 75th percentile warrants closer feeding review.

Percent loss = (birth weight − current weight) ÷ birth weight × 100.

Spit-up vs vomiting
  • Small spit-ups and mucusy gagging in the first 24 h are common.
  • Projectile vomiting after weeks 2–6 suggests pyloric stenosis (not usually a nursery issue).
Bilious (green) emesis = malrotation with volvulus until proven otherwise. Hold feeds and notify the provider immediately.

Emergencies

The fast-recall version. Your NRP card and unit protocols take priority.

NRP flow 2025 update
  1. Before birth: GA? Fluid clear? How many babies? Risk factors? Umbilical cord plan? Check equipment.
  2. Term, good tone, breathing or crying? Yes → skin-to-skin with mom, dry, ongoing assessment. Defer cord clamping ≥60 s for babies who don't need immediate resuscitation.
  3. No: warm, dry, stimulate, position airway. Suction only if the airway is visibly blocked or PPV is needed.
  4. Apnea, gasping, or HR <100 within 1 min → start PPV, SpO₂ monitor, consider ECG. Rate 40–60 breaths/min (2025 guidance accepts 30–60). Rising HR is the main sign that PPV is working.
  5. Starting O₂: 21% for ≥35 weeks. 21–30% for 32–34 6/7 wk. Higher for <32 wk.
  6. HR not rising → MR. SOPA: Mask adjust, Reposition airway, Suction, Open mouth, Pressure increase, Alternative airway (ETT or laryngeal mask).
  7. HR <60 after 30 s of effective PPV (preferably through an alternative airway) → chest compressions, 100% O₂. Two-thumb technique, lower third of the sternum, 1/3 chest depth. 3:1 ratio = 90 compressions + 30 breaths/min.
  8. HR still <60 after 60 s of compressions → epinephrine IV/IO (UVC preferred). Consider volume.

ETT depth: nasal-tragus length + 1 cm, or use the GA table on the NRP card. Laryngeal mask is an option for ≥34 weeks.

Hypoglycemia response
  1. Confirm with POC and send a serum glucose. Don't wait for the result to treat.
  2. Asymptomatic: dextrose gel 0.5 mL/kg (if in protocol) + feed. Recheck in 30–60 min per protocol.
  3. Symptomatic, or glucose below the IV threshold after feeding: notify the provider, place a PIV, D10W 2 mL/kg bolus, then continuous infusion.
  4. Keep the baby warm. Hypothermia worsens hypoglycemia.

A Silverman-Andersen score (0–10: chest movement, intercostal retraction, xiphoid retraction, nasal flaring, grunting) helps track trends.

Sepsis signs

Often vague and subtle: temperature instability (low more often than high), poor feeding, lethargy, irritability, tachypnea, grunting, apnea, tachycardia, mottling, delayed cap refill, hypoglycemia, jaundice.

"Just not acting right" is a legitimate reason to escalate. Get a full set of vitals and a glucose, then call.
Other must-not-miss
  • Bilious emesis: NPO, notify immediately (volvulus).
  • Subgaleal hemorrhage: growing boggy scalp, tachycardia, pallor. Serial head circumference and call.
  • Seizure: protect, time it, support airway, check glucose, notify.
  • Choking or apnea: position, suction mouth then nose, stimulate, PPV if not breathing.
  • Hypothermia: skin-to-skin or warmer, check glucose.

Discharge readiness

Checklist
  • Vitals stable for at least 12 h before discharge
  • Voided and stooled at least once
  • At least 2 successful feeds; weight loss acceptable
  • Newborn metabolic screen drawn (24–48 h)
  • Hearing screen done (OAE or ABR). Follows the 1-3-6 guideline: screen by 1 mo, diagnose by 3 mo, intervene by 6 mo
  • CCHD screen passed
  • Bilirubin assessed with a follow-up plan based on the AAP 2022 curves
  • Hep B and RSV antibody given or declined per policy
  • Car seat screen if indicated; car seat present
  • Circumcision site checked, void after circ
  • Follow-up appointment within 1–2 days if discharged before 72 h of age (sooner if bili risk)
Parent teaching Updated
  • Safe sleep (AAP 2022): Alone, on the Back, in a Crib or bassinet with a firm flat surface. Room-share without bed-sharing. No inclined sleepers, bumpers, pillows or weighted sleep sacks. Inclined sleepers and crib bumpers are banned from sale under the 2022 Safe Sleep for Babies Act.
  • Period of PURPLE crying and never shaking a baby.
  • Rectal temp ≥38.0 °C (100.4 °F) in a baby under 2 months needs immediate medical care.
  • Feeding and diaper expectations, jaundice warning signs, cord care (keep dry, fold diaper below).
  • Car seat: rear-facing, harness snug at or below the shoulders, chest clip at armpit level.

What's changed since about 2019

Practice updates a returning nurse is most likely to notice.

The list
  • Bilirubin (AAP 2022): new phototherapy and exchange curves by GA and risk factors, with generally higher thresholds. The Bhutani zones are no longer used to decide treatment.
  • RSV prevention: nirsevimab (2023) and clesrovimab (2025) are given to most newborns during RSV season, often in the nursery. Maternal RSV vaccine (Abrysvo) at 32–36 weeks is the alternative.
  • Hep B birth dose: in Dec 2025 the CDC dropped the universal birth-dose recommendation for babies of HBsAg-negative mothers. AAP still recommends it. Check your unit's policy.
  • CCHD screening (2020): one repeat screen instead of two.
  • NRP: 8th edition (2021), then 2025 AHA/AAP guidelines and the NRP 9th edition. Emphasis on cord clamping ≥60 s, no routine suction, HR as the main feedback, laryngeal mask for ≥34 wk, IO access acceptable.
  • Neonatal withdrawal: Eat, Sleep, Console has largely replaced Finnegan scoring, with parents at the center of care.
  • Early-onset sepsis: wider use of the Kaiser EOS calculator and fewer empiric antibiotics for well-appearing babies.
  • Hypoglycemia: 40% dextrose gel is now standard first-line in many nurseries.
  • Safe sleep (AAP 2022): stronger language against weighted products and inclined sleepers.
  • Infusion standards (INS 2021): attempt limits, visualization tech (vein finders, ultrasound) encouraged.
  • Congenital syphilis: sharp rise in cases; more maternal testing at delivery.
